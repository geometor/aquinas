Executive Thesis
----------------

In his *Expositio libri Posteriorum Analyticorum* (c. 1270–1272), Thomas Aquinas articulates his mature architecture of demonstrative science (*scientia*), taking geometry and arithmetic not merely as illustrative examples but as the primary realizations of the *most certain mode of demonstration* (*certissimus modus demonstrationis*, Lib. I, lect. 1). Because mathematical entities abstract from :term:`sensible matter <materia-sensibilis>` and motion while retaining intelligible extension or discrete multitude, their demonstrations proceed solely from formal principles in which what is better known *in relation to us* (*quoad nos*) coincides with what is prior and better known *according to nature* (*secundum naturam*, Lib. I, lect. 4). Within this resolutory (*analytica*) framework, Aquinas resolves :person:`Plato <plato>`\ 's *Meno* paradox by distinguishing actual scientific knowledge (*scire simpliciter*) from the virtual pre-existence of conclusions within foreknown principles (*praecognitio*, Lib. I, lect. 2–3). Every demonstrative science requires three coordinated elements: a *subject-genus* (*genus subiectum*, such as continuous magnitude or number, of which both *what it is* and *that it is* are supposed), *proper attributes* (*passiones*, of which only nominal definitions are initially assumed while their inherence is demonstrated), and *common axioms* or *dignities* (*dignitates*, *communes animi conceptiones*, applied proportionally to the :term:`subject-genus <genus-subiectum>`, Lib. I, lect. 18). Crucially, Aquinas identifies :person:`Euclid <euclid>`\ 's constructive problems—such as constructing an equilateral triangle upon a given finite straight line—as *quasi-operative demonstrations* (*demonstrationes quasi operativae*, Lib. I, lect. 2): in the order of construction the figure's mathematical existence is first demonstrated as a *proper attribute* of prior spatial elements, after which the constructed figure serves as a secondary *subject* for subsequent theorems, while singular diagrams such as a figure inscribed in a semicircle immediately yield actual science once recognized under their universal species (Lib. I, lect. 2–3).

Equally foundational is Aquinas's demarcation of the epistemological status of principles in Lectures 5 and 17–19. Whereas *axioms* or *common conceptions of the mind*—such as *If equals be subtracted from equals, the remainders are equal*—are self-evident both in themselves and to every intellect by inward reason alone, *positions* encompass unprovable nominal or quidditative definitions alongside propositional *hypotheses* (*suppositiones*) and *postulates* (*petitiones*), which are demonstrable in a higher science (ultimately first philosophy or natural philosophy) yet assumed without middle terms within geometry according to the learner's epistemic disposition (Lib. I, lect. 5, 17, 19). Against sophistical kind-crossing (*metabasis*), Aquinas exposes Bryson's attempted squaring of the circle as pseudo-demonstrative because its middle term exceeds the proximate genus of circle and rectilinear polygon (Lib. I, lect. 17). Against ancient skeptics and modern empiricists alike, he defends the exactitude of geometry in his celebrated analysis of *lines drawn in the dust* (*lineae in pulvere descriptae*, Lib. I, lect. 19): when the geometer calls a traced mark straight or one foot long, he utters no falsehood because he demonstrates neither *concerning* nor *from* sensible particulars, using visible figures solely as exemplars through which the intellect grasps universal natures existing *one in many* without needing Platonic separated Forms.

Finally, in the closing lectures of Book I and Book II, Aquinas unifies the downward apodictic hierarchy of mathematical exactitude with the upward inductive-abstractive genesis of first principles. Within the mathematical disciplines—ranked internally in Book I (lect. 41–42) by ontological simplicity from arithmetic's *unit without position* (*unitas sine positione*) to geometry's *point having position* (*punctus habens positionem*)—necessity holds *a priori* and universally, excluding both fortuitous events (*a fortuna*) and mere sensory observation (*per sensum*, Lib. I, lect. 42). Even if an observer stood upon the moon witnessing the earth's shadow or perceived by sight that a singular triangle's angles equal two right angles, he would still lack *scientia* until demonstration revealed the universal *per se* cause. Yet in Book II, Lecture 20, Aquinas shows that these universal principles are neither innate Platonic reminiscences nor arbitrary formalist conventions: from repeated sensations retained in memory, human collative reason forms *experience* (*experimentum*), from which the :term:`agent intellect <intellectus-agens>` abstracts the *universal resting in the soul* and awakens *understanding* (*intellectus*)—the infallible *habit of principles* that anchors all demonstrative science.

Key Scholastic & Mathematical Concepts
--------------------------------------

.. list-table::
   :header-rows: 1
   :widths: 22 22 16 40

   * - Latin Term
     - English Rendering
     - Primary Locus
     - Doctrinal & Geometric Significance
   * - *Certissimus modus demonstrationis*
     - Most certain mode of demonstration
     - ``Lib. I, lect. 1 & lect. 4``
     - Designates the epistemic primacy of the mathematical sciences among human disciplines: because mathematics abstracts from sensible matter and argues strictly through formal principles, its premises are simultaneously better known to us and prior in nature.
   * - *Praecognitio (quia est / quid est)*
     - Foreknowledge (that it is / what it is)
     - ``Lib. I, lect. 2``
     - Establishes the asymmetric prerequisites of demonstration: principles require foreknowledge *that* they are true, proper attributes require foreknowledge solely of *what* their names signify, and primary subjects (such as the unit or magnitude) require foreknowledge of both *what* they are and *that* they exist.
   * - *Demonstratio quasi operativa*
     - Quasi-operative demonstration
     - ``Lib. I, lect. 2 & lect. 18``
     - Aquinas's technical characterization of Euclidean constructive propositions (such as *Elements* I.1 and I.46), which demonstrate the mathematical existence (*quia est*) of composite figures like the equilateral triangle and square from presupposed lines and circles before proving further attributes of them.
   * - *Dignitates / Communes animi conceptiones*
     - Axioms / Common conceptions of the mind
     - ``Lib. I, lect. 5 & lect. 18–19``
     - First indemonstrable propositions whose terms (such as *being*, *whole*, *part*, and *equal*) are grasped by all minds; they are evident to inward reason without external syllogism and are employed in geometry and arithmetic proportionally to their respective subject-genera.
   * - *Suppositiones*
     - Hypotheses / Suppositions
     - ``Lib. I, lect. 5 & lect. 19``
     - Propositional principles asserting *to be* or *not to be* that are assumed without proof within a particular science (though provable by a higher science such as metaphysics or natural philosophy) and are readily assented to as probable by the learner.
   * - *Petitiones*
     - Postulates
     - ``Lib. I, lect. 5 & lect. 19``
     - Assumed propositional or constructive principles—exemplified by Euclid's postulate to draw a straight line from any point to any point—which the demonstrator asks the learner to grant when the learner holds neither prior assent nor contrary opinion.
   * - *Genus subiectum*
     - Subject-genus
     - ``Lib. I, lect. 17–18``
     - The unified ontological domain of a science—discrete number for arithmetic, continuous magnitude for geometry—whose essential principles ground *propter quid* demonstrations and forbid illicit kind-crossing (*metabasis*) from alien genera.
   * - *Passiones (per se accidentia)*
     - Proper attributes
     - ``Lib. I, lect. 2, lect. 17 & lect. 18``
     - Properties that flow *per se* and necessarily from the essence of the subject-genus (such as evenness and oddness in numbers, or commensurability, curvature, and angle-sum equality in magnitudes), defined with essential reference to their subject.
   * - *Lineae in pulvere descriptae*
     - Lines drawn in the dust
     - ``Lib. I, lect. 19``
     - Refutes the empiricist and skeptical charge that geometry rests on false premises because drawn diagrams are neither perfectly straight nor exactly a foot long: sensible figures serve solely as phantasmatic exemplars, not as the logical premises or subjects of geometric proof.
   * - *Unitas sine positione / Punctus habens positionem*
     - Unit without position / Point having position
     - ``Lib. I, lect. 41–42``
     - Captures the Aristotelian criterion of relative scientific priority by addition (*ex additione*): whereas the arithmetical unit is indivisible quantity without spatial site, the geometrical point adds determinate position in the continuum, ordering the *a priori* necessity of the mathematical disciplines.
   * - *Experimentum*
     - Experience
     - ``Lib. I, lect. 42 & Lib. II, lect. 20``
     - The collative synthesis of multiple singular memories by human particular reason, forming the indispensable cognitive bridge between transient sensation and the agent intellect's abstraction of the universal resting in the soul.
   * - *Intellectus (habitus principiorum)*
     - Understanding (habit of principles)
     - ``Lib. II, lect. 20``
     - The supreme, unerring intellectual habit—distinct from discursive *scientia*—whereby the human mind, illuminated by the agent intellect upon the data of sense and experience, immediately apprehends first indemonstrable principles.
