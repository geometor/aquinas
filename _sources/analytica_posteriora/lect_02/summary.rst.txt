Summary — On the Twofold Foreknowledge (That It Is and What It Is) of Subject and Attribute: Unit and Triangle
==============================================================================================================

.. rubric:: *Analytical Synopsis & Core Theses of Lib. I, lect. 2*

Argument Summary
----------------

Expositing :person:`Aristotle <aristotle>`\ 's doctrine of *foreknowledge* (*praecognitio*), Aquinas analyzes both *what* must be known prior to a scientific conclusion and the *order* in which it is known. Because every demonstrative conclusion predicates a *proper attribute* (*passio*) of a *subject* through a *principle*, foreknowledge distributes asymmetrically across the two fundamental questions *that it is* (*quia est*) and *what it is* (*quid est*). Principles, being complex enunciations (such as the principle of excluded middle), have no quidditative definition and require foreknowledge solely *that* they are true. Proper attributes cannot have their inherence (*quia est*) foreknown—for proving their inherence is the very task of demonstration—so one foreknows of them only *what is signified by the name* (*quid est quod dicitur*). Primary subjects, such as the *unit* in arithmetic or lines and surfaces in geometry, require foreknowledge of both *what* they are and *that* they exist.

To explain why Aristotle illustrates nominal foreknowledge of a proper attribute using the *triangle*, Aquinas introduces a pivotal distinction between sciences of substance and sciences of accidents like mathematics. In geometry, primary elements of quantity (unit, line, surface) are presupposed exclusively as subjects; from them, composite figures such as the equilateral triangle and the square are first demonstrated to exist through *quasi-operative demonstrations* (quoting :person:`Euclid <euclid>` I.1), where the triangle stands as a *proper attribute*, before subsequently serving as a *subject* for further theorems. Finally, Aquinas distinguishes principles foreknown *prior in time* (the universal major premise that every triangle has three angles equal to two right angles) from truths known *simultaneously in time* yet prior in nature—as when recognizing *this figure inscribed in a semicircle* as a triangle immediately yields scientific knowledge of its angle-sum without any further middle term.

Key Theses
----------

* Demonstrative foreknowledge divides asymmetrically: principles require *that they are true*, proper attributes require *what the name signifies*, and primary subjects require both *what they are* and *that they are*.
* In mathematical sciences of quantity, composite figures like the equilateral triangle and square first function as *proper attributes* proved to exist via *quasi-operative* constructions before serving as secondary *subjects*.
* Whereas the subject is defined absolutely, a proper attribute is defined with essential dependence upon its subject, which supplies the middle term of demonstration.
* When a singular or subordinate instance—such as a figure inscribed in a semicircle—is immediately recognized under its universal species, the minor premise and the demonstrative conclusion become known simultaneously in time.
