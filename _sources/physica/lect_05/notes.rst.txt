Commentary & Notes — On the Perfection of Three-Dimensional Body and the Geometers' Mode of Speaking of a Moving Point
======================================================================================================================

.. rubric:: *Aristotelian Sources, Euclidean Counterparts, Hoenen's Noetics, and GEOMETOR Significance*

Aristotelian & Scholastic Foundations
-------------------------------------

Commenting on the opening argument of *De Caelo* I.1 (268a1–268b10), Aquinas harmonizes the formal definition of the :term:`continuum <continuum>` in *Categories* 6 (5a1–2: parts joined at a common boundary, *pros hena koinon horon*) with the material definition in *Physics* III.1 and VI.1 (*eis aei diaireta diaireton*) to establish corporeal three-dimensionality. Particularly noteworthy is Aquinas's critical methodological restraint in n. 7, drawn directly from :person:`Simplicius <simplicius>`\ 's *Commentary on De Caelo* (ed. Heiberg, 6–9; translated into Latin by William of Moerbeke in 1271, immediately utilized by Aquinas): recognizing that :person:`Aristotle <aristotle>`\ 's appeal to Pythagorean triads, religious libations, and linguistic usage (*ambo* vs. *omnes*) is dialectically probable rather than apodictic, Aquinas cites Claudius :person:`Ptolemy <ptolemy>`\ 's lost treatise *On Dimensionality* (*Peri diastaseos*, preserved in Simplicius), which proves geometrically that at most three mutually perpendicular straight lines can intersect at a single point. Equally incisive is n. 9, explaining Aristotle's remark on impossibility of *metabasis eis allo genos* beyond solid body (268b1–5) by identifying the fluxion of point into line, line into surface, and surface into body as an imaginative idiom of geometers (cf. *De Anima* I.4, 409a3–5).

Euclidean & Classical Geometric Counterparts
--------------------------------------------

Aquinas's synthesis in *In I De Caelo*, lect. 2 bridges the planar geometry of *Elements* Books I–VI and the solid stereometry of Books XI–XIII. :person:`Ptolemy <ptolemy>`\ 's three-perpendiculars proof cited by Aquinas in n. 7 rests directly on *Elements* Book XI Propositions 4–6 and Definition 3–4 (a straight line perpendicular to a plane is at right angles to all straight lines meeting it in that plane; and through a given point on a plane only one perpendicular straight line can be erected, exhausting orthogonal dimensionality at three). Simultaneously, Aquinas's careful gloss on the *modus loquendi geometrarum*—that a moving point makes a line, a moving line a surface, and a moving surface a solid—illuminates both the classical Greek tradition reported by Proclus (*In Eucl.* 97.6–13) and Heron of Alexandria, and :person:`Euclid <euclid>`\ 's own kinematic definitions of the sphere, cone, and cylinder in *Elements* Book XI Definitions 14, 18, and 21 (generated by rotating a semicircle, right triangle, and rectangle around a fixed axis), clarifying that constructive generation expresses dimensional dependency without violating the non-atomistic :term:`continuum <continuum>` of *Physics* VI.1.

Hoenen's De Noetica Geometriae & Judgment Theory
------------------------------------------------

Peter :person:`Hoenen <hoenen>` S.J. cites *In I De Caelo*, lect. 2, n. 9 verbatim in *De Noetica Geometriae* (Chapter IV, § 2, p. 128 and fn. 9, paired with *In II De Caelo*, lect. 2, n. 11 and *In IV Phys.*, lect. 18, n. 4) when evaluating kinematic descriptions in pure geometry. Hoenen shows that Aquinas strikes the exact balance between static ontological rigor and dynamic constructive fruitfulness: strictly speaking, a line cannot be *defined* quidditatively through a moving point without circularity (since motion already presupposes an extended path along which the point moves, as Hoenen demonstrated in Chapter III, § 1), which is why Aquinas attributes this idiom to the *imagination* of geometers (*imaginantes quod punctus motus facit lineam*). Yet as a genetic *description* for tracing special curves (such as Archimedes' spiral or Hippias's quadratrix, where time enters as an auxiliary parameter and is subsequently eliminated to leave a pure geometric locus), kinematic imagination is completely legitimate because it rests on the intelligible continuity and directionality of three-dimensional extension—and in Chapter III, § 2.3, Hoenen likewise echoes Aquinas and :person:`Ptolemy <ptolemy>` on the intuitive clarity of three dimensions against uncritical four-dimensional spatial speculation.

GEOMETOR Constructive & Symbolic Significance
---------------------------------------------

Aquinas's dual insight in *In I De Caelo*, lect. 2—the completeness of three-dimensional orthogonal extension and the distinction between ontological :term:`continuum <continuum>` structure and the constructive *mode of speaking* of geometers—maps directly onto the architecture of GEOMETOR. In ``geometor.explorer`` and ``geometor.model``, constructions unfold sequentially in time as animated sweeps of straightedges and compasses across the canvas, appealing to the geometer's constructive imagination (*modus loquendi geometrarum*) to reveal how complex harmonic configurations emerge step by step from simple seeds. Yet beneath that kinematic animation lies the timeless algebraic continuum of SymPy equations, in which every locus exists complete in its formal definition rather than being assembled out of discrete motion-steps. Furthermore, the culmination of three-dimensional completeness studied in *De Caelo* I.2 points directly toward ``geometor.pacioli`` and *Elements* Book XIII, where planar golden-ratio sections ascend into the five regular Platonic solids—above all the dodecahedron and icosahedron—inscribed within the complete three-dimensional sphere.
