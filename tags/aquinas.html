On Democritus’s Account of Motion and How Geometers Imagine a Moving Point Describing a Line

[lib. 1 l. 11 n. 1 · Marietti #168] After the Philosopher refuted the opinion of those who said without qualification that the soul is moved, here he consequently argues against the opinion of Xenocrates, who added something further beyond motion, namely, that the soul is a self-moving number. And this opinion is much more unreasonable than the opinions of the other philosophers that have been mentioned. For many impossibilities attend those who say that the soul is a self-moving number. First, indeed, there are those that arise from being moved, that is, from motion, which also befall all who say that the soul is moved; and there also befall those who speak in this way proper incongruities of their own, namely from the fact that they say that the soul is a number. And on account of this, the Philosopher refutes this definition of the soul given by Xenocrates, and he is arguing not merely against the wording, but against the very intention of the one defining. Concerning this he does two things. First, he shows that this definition is incongruous with respect to the soul itself or the substance of the soul; second, with respect to its accidents, at Comprehending them together into one, and so forth. Concerning the first he does two things. For first he demolishes the aforesaid definition of the soul by arguments; second, he shows that upon this definition follow all the incongruities that follow upon the opinions of the other philosophers, at Moreover, there befalls, as we have said, and so forth.

On the Division of Per Se Being into Ten Categories and Being Signified by Propositional Judgment

[lib. 5 l. 9 n. 1 · Marietti #885] Here the Philosopher distinguishes in how many ways being is spoken of. And concerning this he does three things. First, he divides being into being per se and being per accidens. Second, he distinguishes the modes of being per accidens, where he says, According to accident indeed, and so forth. Third, the modes of being per se, where he says, According to itself, however. He says, therefore, that one kind of being is spoken of according to itself, and another according to accident. Yet it must be known that this division of being is not the same as that division whereby being is divided into substance and accident. This is clear from the fact that he afterward divides being according to itself into the ten categories, nine of which belong to the genus of accident. Being, therefore, is divided into substance and accident according to the absolute consideration of being, just as whiteness itself considered in itself is called an accident, and man a substance. But being according to accident, as it is taken here, must be grasped by comparing the accident to the substance. And this comparison is signified by the verb is, when it is said, “a man is white.” Hence this whole, “a man is white,” is a being per accidens. Thus it is clear that the division of being according to itself and according to accident is taken according as something is predicated of something either per se or per accidens. The division of being into substance and accident, however, is taken according to the fact that something in its own nature is either a substance or an accident.

On the Necessity of Logic and the Most Certain Mode of Demonstration in the Mathematical Sciences

[lib. 1 l. 1 n. 1] As Aristotle says at the beginning of the Metaphysics, the human race lives by art and reasonings: in saying which the Philosopher seems to touch upon a certain property of man whereby he differs from the other animals. For other animals are impelled to their acts by a certain natural instinct, whereas man is directed in his actions by the judgment of reason. And hence it is that various arts serve to bring human acts to completion easily and in an orderly manner. For art seems to be nothing other than a certain ordering of reason as to how human acts may arrive at their due end through determinate means. Now reason is not only able to direct the acts of the lower powers, but is also directive of its own act. For this is proper to the intellective part, that it reflect upon itself: for the intellect understands itself, and similarly reason can reason about its own act. If, therefore, from the fact that reason reasons about the act of the hand there was devised the art of building or of carpentry, through which man can exercise acts of this kind easily and in an orderly manner, by the same reason a certain art is necessary that is directive of the very act of reason itself, through which, namely, man may proceed in the very act of reason in an orderly manner, easily, and without error.

On Parts of the Form vs. Parts of Matter: Bronze Circle and Mathematical Circle in Intelligible Matter

[lib. 7 l. 10 n. 1 · Marietti #1482] Because the solution set forth above was not in every respect manifest—for he had not yet shown how parts are prior and posterior, nor again had he distinguished the universal composite from the particular, nor even the species from the form—he therefore here explains in detail the solution set forth above. Now this text is divided into two parts. In the first, he explains the solution set forth above; in the second, he teaches how it is to be applied to the question, where he says, One must meet the interrogation, however. The first is divided into two. First, he solves the question with respect to what was asked concerning the priority of parts; second, with respect to what was asked concerning whether the parts of the thing defined enter into the definition, where he says, But the parts of the formula. The first is divided into two. First, he shows how parts are prior to the whole; second, he makes this manifest by an example, where he says, Since, however. On the Origin of Science from Sensible Things, Turning to Phantasms, and Threefold Abstraction

[I q. 84 a. 6 arg. 1] We proceed thus to the sixth article. It seems that intellectual knowledge is not received from sensible things. For Augustine says in the Book of Eighty-Three Questions that the pureness of truth is not to be expected from the senses of the body. And he proves this in two ways. In one way, from the fact that everything that bodily sense reaches is changed without any intermission of time, and what does not abide cannot be perceived. In another way, from the fact that all things that we sense through the body, even when they are not present to the senses, nevertheless leave us subject to their images, as in sleep or frenzy; yet by the senses we are unable to discern whether we are sensing the sensible objects themselves or their false images. Now nothing can be perceived that is not discerned from the false. And thus he concludes that truth is not to be expected from the senses. Now intellectual knowledge is apprehensive of truth. Therefore intellectual knowledge is not to be expected from the senses.

On Universal Knowledge and Recognizing the Singular Triangle Inscribed in a Semicircle

[lib. 1 l. 3 n. 1] After the Philosopher has shown how certain other things must be foreknown before knowledge of the conclusion is acquired, he now wishes to show how it happens that the conclusion itself is foreknown before knowledge of it is acquired through syllogism or induction. And concerning this he does two things. First, he determines the truth of the matter, saying that before induction or syllogism is introduced to produce knowledge of some conclusion, that conclusion is in a certain manner known, and in a certain manner not known: for unqualifiedly it is unknown, yet it is known only in a qualified sense. For instance, if this conclusion is to be proved, A triangle has three angles equal to two right angles, before it was demonstrated, he who receives scientific knowledge of it through demonstration did not know it unqualifiedly, yet knew it in a qualified sense. Hence in a certain manner he foreknew it, though not unqualifiedly. Now the reason for this is that, as has already been shown, the principles must be foreknown prior to the conclusion. And principles stand to conclusions in demonstrative matters just as active causes in natural things stand to their effects (whence in Book II of the Physics the propositions of a syllogism are placed in the genus of efficient cause). Now before an effect is produced in act, it pre-exists in its active causes virtually, though not in act, which is to exist unqualifiedly. And similarly, before a conclusion is deduced from demonstrative principles, in those very foreknown principles the conclusion is foreknown virtually, though not in act: for in this way it pre-exists in them. And thus it is clear that it is not foreknown unqualifiedly, but in a qualified sense.

[pars 3 q. 5 a. 3 arg. 1] We proceed thus to the third article. It seems that mathematical consideration is not without matter concerning things that have their being in matter. On Discovering Geometric Truth by Dividing Figures in Act (Euclid I.32 and III.31)

[lib. 9 l. 10 n. 1 · Marietti #1883] After the Philosopher has compared act and potency with respect to the prior and the posterior, he here compares them with respect to good and evil; and concerning this he does two things. First, he says that in good things, act is better than potency. And this is manifest from the fact that what is in potency is the selfsame thing existing in potency to contraries: just as that which is able to recover health is able to fall sick, and is simultaneously in potency to both. And this is because there is the selfsame potency for both—for recovering health and for ailing, for resting and for moving, and for other opposites of this sort. Taken separately, therefore, of the two contraries the one is good, such as the healthy, while the other is evil, such as the sick; for among contraries one always stands as deficient, which pertains to evil.

On Positional Directions in Natural Bodies and How They Are Considered by Position in Mathematical Figures

And this seems worthy of consideration because right from the outset it occurs to anyone that, if right and left exist in the heaven, much more and with greater priority must the prior principles—namely above and below, before and behind—be judged to exist in the heaven.

Whether in Divine Things Imagination Must Be Altogether Relinquished (Why Mathematical Judgment Terminates at the Imagination)

[pars 3 q. 6 a. 2 arg. 1] We proceed thus to the second article. It seems that in divine things one must be led to imaginations. For divine science is never handed down more fittingly than in Sacred Scripture. Yet in Sacred Scripture, when treating of divine things, we are led to imaginations, inasmuch as divine things are described to us under sensible figures. Therefore in divine things one must be led to imaginations.

That No Particular Science Demonstrates Its Own Proper Principles, Which Belong to First Philosophy

[lib. 1 l. 17 n. 1] Above, the Philosopher had shown that demonstration does not proceed from extraneous principles; here, however, he shows that it does not proceed from common principles alone. And concerning this he does two things: first, he proves his proposition; second, he draws a certain conclusion from what has been said, at Now if this is manifest, et cetera. Concerning the first he does three things. First, he states his intention, saying that because it is manifest that it is impossible to demonstrate each thing through anything whatsoever, rather demonstration must proceed from the principles of each thing in such a way that what is demonstrated belongs to it insofar as it is that thing—that is, the principles of demonstration must belong per se to that which is demonstrated; if, I say, this is so, then in order for something to be scientifically known it does not suffice that it be demonstrated from true and immediate premises, but it must furthermore be demonstrated from proper principles.

On the Three Elements Required in Every Demonstrative Science: Subject-Genus, Proper Attributes, and Common Axioms

[lib. 1 l. 18 n. 1] After he has shown that demonstration proceeds not from common principles alone, but from proper principles, here, in order to make clear what has preceded, he determines the nature of proper and common principles. And concerning this he does two things. First, he shows the necessity of this determination, saying that it is difficult to recognize whether we know from proper principles (which alone is truly to know scientifically) or not from proper principles. For many suppose themselves to know scientifically whenever they possess a syllogism drawn from certain premises that are true and primary. Yet this is not true; rather, in order for us to know scientifically, the principles must be proximate to the things that are to be demonstrated (which are here called primary, just as above they were called extremes), or else proximate to the first indemonstrable principles.

How One Science Is More Certain and Prior to Another: The Unit Without Position and the Point Having Position

[lib. 1 l. 42 n. 1] After the Philosopher has assigned the reason for the unity and diversity of the sciences on the part of the subject-genus and on the part of the principles, here he pursues both further: and first, concerning the subjects of which scientific knowledge is had; second, concerning the principles, at Now whether the principles are the same, et cetera. Concerning the first, it must be considered that above he had laid down two conditions for the genus that serves as the subject of a science: one of which is that it be composed of primary elements, and the other is that its parts and proper attributes belong to it per se. Now one of these conditions is lacking in things that happen by fortune, because they do not come about per se, but per accidens and beside intention, as is proved in Book II of the Physics. The other condition is lacking in the things known through sense, even though they come first in our cognition. And therefore, first, he shows that there is no scientific knowledge of things that happen by fortune; second, he shows that there is no scientific knowledge of things insofar as they are known through sense, at Nor is it possible to know scientifically through sense, et cetera.
