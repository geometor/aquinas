Commentary & GEOMETOR Notes — PART III — COMMENTARY ON ARISTOTLE’S DE ANIMA¶

Aristotelian Sources, Euclidean Counterparts, Hoenen’s Noetics, and Symbolic Constructive Geometry

Book I, Lecture 11 — On Democritus’s Account of Motion and How Geometers Imagine a Moving Point Describing a Line¶

Primary Text: English (Lib. I, lect. 11) · Latin (Lib. I, lect. 11) · Summary

Aristotelian & Scholastic Foundations¶

Aquinas comments here on \ Aristotle‘s De Anima I.4 (408b32–409b18), continuing the critique of kinetic theories of the soul begun at I.3 (406b15–25, where \ Democritus‘s spherical fire-atoms and \ Plato‘s Timaeus circles were first paired). Xenocrates, scholarch of the Old Academy after Speusippus, sought to synthesize Platonic mathematical metaphysics with kinetic psychology by defining the soul as ‘number moving itself’ (ἀριθμὸς κινῶν ἑαυτόν). Following Aristotle’s cross-references to Physics VI (an indivisible cannot move per se, nor compose a \ continuum) and Physics VIII (every self-mover divides into an unmoved or moving part and a moved part), Aquinas exposes a fatal category mistake: once arithmetic units are localized inside an animate body, they acquire spatial position (positio / θέσις) and become indistinguishable either from geometric points or from Democritus’s corpuscular atoms, rendering them incapable of either self-motion or biological formal causality.

Euclidean & Classical Geometric Counterparts¶

Paragraph 170 directly engages the foundational tension in Greek geometry between static essential definitions and genetic or kinematic descriptions. In \ Euclid‘s Elements, Book I, Definitions 1–4 define the point (‘that which has no part’), line (‘breadthless length’), extremities of a line, and straight line purely statically by boundaries and dimensional privation, without invoking motion; yet Postulates 1–3 (‘to draw a straight line from any point to any point,’ ‘to produce a finite straight line continuously,’ ‘to describe a circle with any center and distance’) and Book XI’s solid revolutions (spheres, cones, and cylinders generated by rotating semicircles, triangles, and rectangles) rely on kinematic construction. Proclus (In Euclidem, p. 97 and p. 185, citing Geminus) preserves the Academic characterization of the line as the ‘flux of a point’ (ῥύσις τοῦ σημείου) and the straight line as an ‘even and undeviating flux.’ Aquinas’s exposition clarifies why Euclid excluded motion from Book I’s essential definitions of point and line while retaining constructive postulates: motion belongs to the geometer’s imaginative generation of figures, whereas ontological priority in the continuum proceeds from solid body down to bounding surface, line, and point (no. 174).

Hoenen’s De Noetica Geometriae & Judgment Theory¶

In De Noetica Geometriae, Chapter IV, § 2 (with footnotes 8 and 9 citing In I De Anima, lect. 11, Pirotta no. 170 alongside In De Caelo I, lect. 2, no. 9 and In Physicorum IV, lect. 18, no. 4), Peter \ Hoenen S.J. highlights Aquinas’s precise epistemological diagnosis: whereas Aristotle’s text at 409a4 simply says ‘they say’ (φασι) that a moved point makes a line—prompting Trendelenburg to wonder whether the Pythagoreans or all geometers were meant—Aquinas identifies the ontological thesis as Platonist here (no. 170) while elsewhere attributing the idiom to ‘geometers imagining (imaginantes) that a moved point makes a line.’ Hoenen uses this distinction to resolve modern objections (such as Bertrand \ Russell‘s and Hermann von Helmholtz’s critiques of superposition and kinematic geometry): geometry does not treat physical efficient causality or temporal velocity, which belong to physics, but legitimately employs imagined continuous motion as a genetic description (not a strict definition) grounded in the homogeneity of spatial extension as intelligible matter and principle of individuation, from which both passive rigid-body mobility and constant direction necessarily flow.

GEOMETOR Constructive & Symbolic Significance¶

Aquinas’s distinction between the arithmetic unit lacking position, the geometric point as a ‘unit having position’ (unitas positionem habens), and the kinematic imagination of a point tracing a line illuminates the core architectural duality of GEOMETOR. Within \ geometor.model, points are not floating-point pixels or Democritean corpuscles of small breadth, but exact algebraic indivisibles endowed with symbolic coordinates—literally exact units possessing position within an algebraic field over the rationals and quadratic surds. Meanwhile, when \ geometor.explorer animates a compass sweep or straightedge extension across the SVG canvas, it enacts precisely the geometer’s kinematic imagination described in Pirotta no. 170: the visual sweep does not constitute the algebraic essence of the line or circle, which exists timelessly in polynomial incidence relations, yet it renders the genetic dependency graph of the construction transparent to human intuition.

Book II, Lecture 16 — On Sound in Act and Potency and Its Fluent Mode of Being in Motion¶

Primary Text: English (Lib. II, lect. 16) · Latin (Lib. II, lect. 16) · Summary

Aristotelian & Scholastic Foundations¶

Commenting on \ Aristotle‘s De Anima II.8 (419b4–420a19), Aquinas situates acoustics within Aristotle’s hierarchy of the external senses: sight perceives through a purely spiritual or intentional immutation of the pellucid medium, whereas hearing perceives through local motion propagated across air or water. Aquinas’s methodological prologue (no. 438) coordinates the entire Aristotelian libri naturales—cross-referencing De Sensu et Sensato for color, flavor, and odor, De Generatione et Corruptione for tangible contraries, and Meteorologica—to explain why sound alone must be analyzed in fieri within De Anima itself: because sound possesses no static accident residing in the bronze bell, its formal actuality is inseparable from the kinetic propagation of percussion through an undivided medium.

Euclidean & Classical Geometric Counterparts¶

Aquinas’s exposition of acoustic propagation and reverberation (nos. 446–449) deploys explicitly Euclidean geometric models of radial symmetry and catoptric reflection. First, the diffusion of compressed air from the locus of percussion is modeled as a family of concentric circular waves (gyrationes) expanding outward from a center (\ Euclid, Elements I, Def. 15), where wave intensity varies inversely with radial distance. Second, Aquinas pairs acoustic echoes from smooth or concave vessels with the optical reflection of light rays from water and polished bronze (aes, rendering Aristotle’s χαλκός at 419b31), invoking the geometric law of reflection systematized in the Euclidean Catoptrics—where incident and reflected rays make equal angles with a smooth surface, while concave surfaces converge and multiply reflected impulses and rough opaque surfaces scatter light diffusely so as to prevent total shadow outside direct solar rays.

Hoenen’s De Noetica Geometriae & Judgment Theory¶

In the metaphysical Appendix to De Noetica Geometriae (‘Additions,’ nos. 1–2, citing In II De Anima, lect. 16 alongside In IV Physicorum, lect. 17 and De Potentia, q. 7, a. 2, ad 9), \ Hoenen elevates Aquinas’s contrast between permanent qualities and sound lacking ‘a fixed and resting esse in the subject’ (no. 438) into a general theory of existential modes of being. Motion strictly speaking possesses a continuous fluent being (esse fluens) whose successive duration and variable velocity exhibit quantitative and structural proper attributes analogous—yet irreducibly heterogeneous—to permanent spatial extension. Tracing a line from Aquinas and Nicole Oresme’s medieval ‘configurations of qualities across temporal duration’ to modern wave mechanics and quantum photons, Hoenen shows how periodic, oscillatory variations in fluent being explain acoustic pitch and optical color without collapsing four-dimensional spacetime calculations into a univocal geometric \ continuum.

GEOMETOR Constructive & Symbolic Significance¶

Aquinas’s contrast between permanent, resting form (esse permanens et fixum) and fluent being (esse fluens) propagating in concentric circular waves directly mirrors the interplay between static structural invariants and dynamic wave-like propagation in GEOMETOR constructions. In \ geometor.model, each compass construction radiates from a struck center across a chosen radial distance, generating new intersection loci step by step along a directed acyclic graph whose expanding rings echo the concentric gyrationes of Lecture 16. While the completed symbolic model retains permanent algebraic being—every golden ratio proportion and harmonic cross-ratio discovered by \ geometor.divine holds timelessly—the constructive sequence animated in \ geometor.explorer unfolds in fluent being, allowing the inquirer to witness how local percussions of point and radius reverberate across the entire geometric field.

Book III, Lecture 8 — On the Object of the Intellect: Flesh and Bone vs. the Continuum, the Snub vs. the Straight, and Reflection upon the Phantasm¶

Primary Text: English (Lib. III, lect. 8) · Latin (Lib. III, lect. 8) · Summary

Aristotelian & Scholastic Foundations¶

Expounding the notoriously terse and contested text of De Anima III.4 (429b5–22), Aquinas weaves together Metaphysics VII (Z.6 and Z.10–11 on essence, individual composites, and \ intelligible matter) with De Anima III to solve how a single immaterial faculty relates to hylomorphic things. \ Aristotle contrasts concrete magnitudes and natural substances (‘water’ vs. ‘to be for water,’ ‘flesh’ vs. ‘to be for flesh’) with mathematical abstractions (‘the straight’ vs. ‘to be for the straight,’ provisionally illustrated by the Academic formula of duality), comparing the straight with the snub (τὸ σιμόν) because both involve a substrate—continuous extension (τὸ συνεχές) in the former, sensible nose in the latter. Against \ Avicenna‘s extrinsic emanationism (nos. 701–702), \ Plato‘s separated Forms and Mathematicals (nos. 704, 716), and \ Averroes‘s single separate possible intellect (no. 718), Aquinas defends an intrinsic human intellect that preserves habitual species, abstracts universal quiddities from individuation, and bends back (circumflexa, 429b16) upon sensory powers to know singulars.

Euclidean & Classical Geometric Counterparts¶

Lecture 8 supplies the ontological warrant for two pillars of \ Euclid‘s Elements: the multiplicity of congruent figures (Book I, Prop. 4 and Common Notion 4) and the definition of straightness within continuous magnitude (Book I, Def. 4). If mathematical forms were wholly separated from matter like Platonic Ideas, there could exist only one unique Triangle-Itself and one unique Circle-Itself, making Euclidean constructions—such as erecting an equilateral triangle upon a given segment by intersecting two distinct circles in Book I, Prop. 1, or comparing two distinct triangles ABC and DEF in Prop. 4—metaphysically impossible. Aquinas’s demonstration that continuous quantity remains in the intellect as intelligible matter (nos. 706–707) explains how numerically distinct triangles share an identical specific essence while differing solely by site within the continuum, and how the straight (no. 713) is essentially an affection of continuous extension.

Hoenen’s De Noetica Geometriae & Judgment Theory¶

In De Noetica Geometriae, Chapter II, § 7 (Note 1) and Chapter IV, §§ 3–4, \ Hoenen identifies De Anima III, Lecture 8 (together with Summa Theologiae I, q. 85, a. 1, ad 2 and Super Boetium De Trinitate, q. 5, a. 3) as the keystone solving both fundamental problems of geometric epistemology: necessity and exactitude. Hoenen emphasizes that in formal abstraction ‘matter’ signifies what is opaque to intellective intuition (sensible qualities), whereas intelligible matter (ὕλη νοητή)—the extended continuum retained by formal abstraction—is thoroughly transparent to the human mind as pure potency for geometric figures. Moreover, Hoenen demonstrates that intuiting this homogeneous continuum purely as the principle of individuation (differentiating figures by site and number alone, Pirotta nos. 705–707) justifies Euclidean congruence without assuming physically rigid bodies (refuting Poincaré) and establishes both the quid est and mathematical existence (an est) of exact straight lines of constant direction and uniform planes.

GEOMETOR Constructive & Symbolic Significance¶

Aquinas’s double doctrine in Lecture 8—intelligible matter individuating geometric species and the bent-line reflection (redit super phantasmata) linking universal \ quiddity to singular instance—provides the exact philosophical blueprint for the coupling of \ geometor.model and \ geometor.explorer. In \ geometor.model, a geometric element is defined free of all sensible matter (stroke width, pixel rasterization, or pigment), yet never as a Platonic form divorced from intelligible matter: distinct lines and circles are individuated within the symbolic continuum by their exact algebraic coordinates and polynomial equations, permitting arbitrarily many congruent circles and Golden Sections under one universal ratio. Conversely, whenever the researcher inspects a specific constructed locus on the \ geometor.explorer canvas and traces it back to its exact SymPy expression, the workflow performs Aquinas’s cognitive reflection—extending straight to grasp the universal algebraic relation and bending back upon the determinate visual phantasm to verify its singular instantiation.

Book III, Lecture 12 — Why the Soul Never Understands Without a Phantasm and How Mathematical Entities Are Understood in Abstraction¶

Primary Text: English (Lib. III, lect. 12) · Latin (Lib. III, lect. 12) · Summary

Aristotelian & Scholastic Foundations¶

Aquinas expounds De Anima III.7 (431a1–b19), a dense dossier of Aristotelian insights uniting cognitive psychology, theory of action, and philosophy of mathematics. Beginning from the crucial distinction at 431a4–7 between physical kinesis (incomplete act of the imperfect) and cognitive energeia or operatio (complete act of the perfected power, nos. 764–765), Aquinas traces \ Aristotle‘s analogy between sensory discrimination and intellectual judgment (431a8–b2), culminating in the celebrated dictum at 431a16–17: διὸ οὐδέποτε νοεῖ ἄνευ φαντάσματος ἡ ψυχή (‘wherefore the soul never understands without a \ phantasm,’ no. 771). Aquinas then unpacks 431b12–19 on τὰ ἐν ἀφαιρέσει λεγόμενα (‘things spoken of in abstraction,’ nos. 780–783), showing how Aristotle replaces Platonic chorismos (ontological separation) with selective attention of the \ agent intellect, which contemplates the curved without flesh while never falsely judging curvature to subsist apart from bodies.

Euclidean & Classical Geometric Counterparts¶

Two explicit mathematical structures govern Aquinas’s commentary in Lecture 12. First, to explain how a single indivisible cognitive power discriminates heterogeneous or contrary qualities without splitting into separate subjects, Aquinas twice invokes the Euclidean circle (Book I, Def. 15): both the common sense and the judging intellect stand as a single unextended center at which multiple radial lines from the external senses and phantasms terminate simultaneously (nos. 767, 772). Second, in paragraph 775, Aquinas formalizes Aristotle’s lettered proportion at 431a25–29 using Book V, Proposition 16 of \ Euclid‘s Elements (permutatio proportionum or alternando): if sensible a (white) stands to sensible b (black) as phantasm c stands to phantasm d, then by alternation a stands to c as b stands to d, proving rigorously that one unified intellect judges c and d just as one unified sense judges a and b. Finally, nos. 780–782 ground the legitimacy of Euclidean geometry’s idealized curves and surfaces: abstracting the curved from sensible flesh involves no mendacity because sensible qualities fall outside curvature’s formal ratio.

Hoenen’s De Noetica Geometriae & Judgment Theory¶

In De Noetica Geometriae, Chapter I, § 2 (explicitly citing De Anima III.7, 431a14–b2 and Aquinas, Lecture 12, Pirotta nos. 770–772 and 777 alongside Posterior Analytics I.12, 77b30) and Chapter III, \ Hoenen demonstrates that mathematical abstraction is an intuition conjoined with abstraction occurring within the phantasm: ταῦτα δ’ ἐστὶν οἷον ὁρᾶν τῇ νοήσει (‘these mathematicals are, as it were, seen by intellection’). Confronting the sensory ‘threshold of exactitude’ (limen exactitudinis)—the fact that neither eye nor imagination can distinguish a true breadthless line or exact concurrency of three medians from a slight deviation—Hoenen shows from Lecture 12 that the intellect does not merely copy blurry phantasms (Mill’s empiricism) nor fabricate axioms a priori apart from experience (modern formalism). Instead, inspecting the concrete phantasm, the agent intellect abstracts formal spatial relations apart from sensible defects (nos. 780–783) and intuits exact, necessary nexus directly in the imagined configuration.

GEOMETOR Constructive & Symbolic Significance¶

Lecture 12 directly addresses the central epistemological paradox of computational geometry: why visual rendering remains indispensable even when symbolic algebra possesses infinite exactitude. Human perception and screen displays are bounded by Hoenen’s limen exactitudinis—finite SVG stroke widths and floating-point viewport coordinates in \ geometor.explorer are sensible approximations—yet ‘the soul cannot understand without a phantasm’ (no. 771). In GEOMETOR, \ geometor.explorer supplies the luminous, structured phantasm in which the geometer visually deliberates and spots candidate collinearities, concurrencies, and golden sections ‘as though actually seeing them’ (no. 777), while \ geometor.model performs the precise non-mendacious abstraction of nos. 780–783: stripping away every pixel approximation to verify in pure symbolic algebra whether curvature, straightness, and harmonic proportion hold per se.

Book III, Lecture 13 — How the Soul Is in a Way All Things, the Hand the Organ of Organs, and the Intellect the Form of Forms¶

Primary Text: English (Lib. III, lect. 13) · Latin (Lib. III, lect. 13) · Summary

Aristotelian & Scholastic Foundations¶

Commenting on De Anima III.8 (431b20–432a14), Aquinas brings \ Aristotle‘s treatise on the cognitive powers to its grand architectural synthesis. Aristotle recapitulates his critique of Pre-Socratic materialism from Book I (such as Empedocles’s physical isomorphism, ‘by earth we see earth,’ 404b13), reinterpreting the ancient insight through hylomorphic intentionality: ἡ ψυχὴ τὰ ὄντα πώς ἐστι πάντα (‘the soul is in a way all beings,’ 431b21) because sense receives sensible forms without matter and intellect receives intelligible forms. At 432a1–3 Aristotle crowns this doctrine with the celebrated proportion: ὥστε ἡ ψυχὴ ὥσπερ ἡ χείρ ἐστιν· καὶ γὰρ ἡ χεὶρ ὄργανόν ἐστιν ὀργάνων, καὶ ὁ νοῦς εἶδος εἰδῶν (‘so that the soul is like the hand; for the hand is the organ of organs, and the intellect is the form of forms,’ no. 789), before insisting at 432a3–14 that because no intelligible exists apart from sensible magnitudes, anyone contemplating in act must simultaneously behold a phantasm (ἅμα φάντασμά τι θεωρεῖν, nos. 790–793).

Euclidean & Classical Geometric Counterparts¶

Aquinas’s insistence in nos. 790–791 that even a master possessing habitual geometry cannot contemplate in act without forming a singular phantasm illuminates the classical formal structure of every proposition in \ Euclid‘s Elements (analyzed by Proclus into protasis, ekthesis, diorismos, kataskeue, apodeixis, and symperasma). Euclid never proves a universal protasis (‘On a given finite straight line to construct an equilateral triangle,’ or ‘In right-angled triangles the square on the hypotenuse equals the squares on the sides’) through imageless verbal syllogisms alone; instead, he immediately performs an ekthesis (‘Let AB be the given finite straight line’) and kataskeue (auxiliary circles and segments), instantiating a singular sensible-imaginative diagram. As Aquinas explains in no. 793, the Euclidean diagram (phantasma) is particular in representation yet contains the universal geometric truth in potency, while the compass and straightedge themselves exemplify manual instruments crafted by the hand as organ of organs (no. 789).

Hoenen’s De Noetica Geometriae & Judgment Theory¶

In De Noetica Geometriae, Chapter I, § 2 (citing De Anima III.8, 432a5–9 and Aquinas, Lecture 13, Pirotta no. 791) and Chapter V (on symbolic logic and logica utens), \ Hoenen deploys Aquinas’s anti-Avicennian argument to critique modern mathematical logicism and Hilbertian pure formalism. Just as \ Avicenna imagined that once the intellect has acquired scientific habits it can operate purely among abstract intentions without returning to sensory imagination—a thesis refuted by Aquinas both ontologically and clinically (no. 791)—radical formalists suppose that once axioms are encoded in ‘transcendent terms’ (symbolic letters a, b, c), geometry dispenses with spatial intuition altogether. Hoenen acknowledges (quoting Hadamard on \ Hilbert) the immense utility of stable symbolic formulas as intellectual instruments, yet proves from Pirotta no. 791 that symbols retain real geometric meaning and truth (which belongs solely to intellectual composition and division, no. 792) only insofar as the mind vitalizes them through ongoing turning to the phantasms.

GEOMETOR Constructive & Symbolic Significance¶

Aquinas’s analogy of the hand as the organ of organs (organum organorum) and the intellect as the form of forms (species specierum, no. 789), paired with the permanent necessity of the phantasm in habitual contemplation (nos. 790–791), articulates the deepest philosophical identity of the GEOMETOR ecosystem. Symbolic computation in \ geometor.model, automated proportional discovery in \ geometor.divine, propositional DAG reconstruction in \ geometor.elements, and interactive visualization in \ geometor.explorer are intellectual extensions of the organ of organs—universal cognitive instruments crafted by human reason in place of fixed biological instincts. Crucially, GEOMETOR refuses the Avicennian or formalist severance of algebra from vision: \ geometor.model never replaces the visual phantasm of \ geometor.explorer, nor is geometric truth reduced to the mere image on the canvas (no. 793); rather, exact symbolic reason and luminous spatial imagination operate in continuous synergy whenever the geometer contemplates in act.
