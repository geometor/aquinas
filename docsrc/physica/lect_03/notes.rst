Commentary & Notes — On the Definition of Immobile Place from Local Translation and Themistius's Solution
=========================================================================================================

.. rubric:: *Aristotelian Sources, Euclidean Counterparts, Hoenen's Noetics, and GEOMETOR Significance*

Aristotelian & Scholastic Foundations
-------------------------------------

Aquinas expounds *Physics* IV.5 (212a31–212b29), where :person:`Aristotle <aristotle>` applies his definition of place—*the first immobile boundary of what contains* (*to tou periechontos peras akinēton prōton*, 212a20)—to the cosmos as a whole. Because the aporia of the outermost sphere's diurnal rotation became one of the fiercest battlegrounds of ancient and medieval natural philosophy, Aquinas stages in nn. 3–9 a magisterial disputation surveying John Philoponus's and the Stoics' three-dimensional void space (*spatium dimensiones habens*), Alexander of Aphrodisias, :person:`Avicenna <avicenna>` (*Sufficientia* II.3, positing motion in *situs*), Avempace / Ibn Bajja (locating a sphere by its inner convex core), :person:`Averroes <averroes>` (*In Phys.* IV, comm. 43–45, positing *per accidens* location via the immobile earth at the center), and :person:`Themistius <themistius>` (*In Phys.* IV, ed. Schenkl, 120–122). Siding decisively with Themistius against Averroes (whom Aquinas even criticizes in n. 15 for forcing Aristotle's text on water), Aquinas shows how Aristotle's distinction between parts existing *potentially* in place within a homogeneous :term:`continuum <continuum>` (*homoimeres*) versus *actually* in place within a *contiguous* heap resolves the paradox without surrendering the relational surface-ontology of place.

Euclidean & Classical Geometric Counterparts
--------------------------------------------

Aquinas's critique of self-subsistent void space (n. 3), :person:`Avicenna <avicenna>`\ 's indivisibility of *situs* (n. 4), Avempace's inner-boundary theory (n. 5), and :person:`Themistius <themistius>`\ 's rotational invariance (nn. 7–14) turns throughout on Euclidean geometry. Against Avempace, Aquinas invokes the Euclidean axioms of containment and equality (*Elements* Common Notion 4: things coinciding with one another are equal to one another): the concave inner surface of a hollow shell is strictly smaller in area than its outer volume and fails to bound it externally. Even more strikingly, Aquinas's analysis in n. 7 of a sphere rotating in place—where the whole sphere remains in the selfsame spatial locus *in subject* while its circularly ordered parts continuously exchange positions—corresponds directly to :person:`Euclid <euclid>`\ 's genetic definition of the sphere in *Elements* Book XI Definition 14 (the figure comprehended when a semicircle revolves around its fixed diameter until it returns to the same position) and underpins the Euclidean method of superposition (*epharmozein*) in *Elements* I.4, I.8, and III.24, which presupposes that spatial translation and rotation neither stretch nor deform a figure's metric determinations.

Hoenen's De Noetica Geometriae & Judgment Theory
------------------------------------------------

In *De Noetica Geometriae*, Appendix § 2 (*Hoc movetur, ergo locus est*, pp. 115–138), Peter :person:`Hoenen <hoenen>` S.J. explicitly cites and transcribes *In IV Physicorum*, lect. 7, n. 7 as a 'beautiful text' (*pulchrum textum*) establishing an intuitive existential nexus against Humean and Kantian skepticism: local translation immediately manifests the existence of place through the succession of bodies within an invariant surrounding extension. Moreover, in Chapter IV (§§ 1–3, pp. 61–158), Hoenen connects :person:`Aristotle <aristotle>`\ 's and Aquinas's doctrine of translation and rotation in *Phys.* IV with the foundation of geometric congruence against Bertrand :person:`Russell <russell>` and Henri Poincaré. Russell objected that moving a triangle in *Elements* I.4 treats spatial points as material particles, while Poincaré claimed geometry depends empirically on physical rigid bodies. Hoenen replies via Aquinas's *materia intelligibilis* and rotational continuity: just as the parts of :person:`Themistius <themistius>`\ 's homogeneous sphere continuously succeed one another in congruent positions without physical deformation, pure extension as the principle of geometric individuation guarantees *a priori* that any figure realized in one site can be identically realized across a continuous succession of sites.

GEOMETOR Constructive & Symbolic Significance
---------------------------------------------

Aquinas's twin doctrines in *In IV Phys.*, lect. 7—that place is not an empty Cartesian pixel-box existing prior to extended bodies, and that circular rotation maps a homogeneous :term:`continuum <continuum>` onto itself in subject while permuting its parts—illuminate how ``geometor.model`` conceives spatial reference without arbitrary grid discretization. In ``geometor.model``, the geometric plane is not a pre-allocated finite array of raster cells; rather, a relational coordinate frame is anchored by two initial foundational points that define the unit interval, from which every subsequent locus is generated by straightedge and compass incidence—an exact computational counterpart to :person:`Aristotle <aristotle>`\ 's principle that local determinations arise only from extended bodies and their mutual boundaries. Likewise, Aquinas's contrast between rectilinear translation (which shifts a whole figure into an extrinsic place) and circular rotation (which cyclically permutes consecutive parts within a self-contained locus) mirrors the interplay of linear extensions and circular compass sweeps in ``geometor.elements`` and ``geometor.explorer``, where rotational symmetry around a center generates invariant regular polygons and golden-angle phyllotactic rings.
