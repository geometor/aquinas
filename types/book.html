Commentary on Boethius’s On the Trinity (Questions V–VI)

Composed at Paris between 1257 and 1259 and preserved in part in his own autograph, Thomas Aquinas’s Expositio super librum Boethii De Trinitate (Questions V and VI) constitutes the most rigorous epistemological treatise of the Latin Middle Ages on the architecture and methodology of the theoretical sciences. Confronting competing Aristotelian, Boethian, Platonic, Avicennian, and Victorine classifications of human knowledge, Aquinas grounds the tripartite division of speculative philosophy—natural science, mathematics, and divine science—not in arbitrary ontological taxonomies, nor merely in subjective psychological faculties, but in the per se differences of speculative objects (speculabilia) precisely insofar as they stand proportioned to an immaterial intellect seeking necessary scientific truth. Because the intellect is immaterial in its power and demonstrative science (scientia) demands immobile necessity in its object, speculative objects are diversified essentially by their grade of removal from matter and motion: physics treats realities that depend upon sensible matter both in being (secundum esse) and in definition (secundum intellectum); mathematics treats realities that depend upon matter in being yet are defined without sensible matter; and divine science treats realities independent of matter and motion in being itself.

St. Thomas Aquinas, O.P. (1225–1274)

Commentary on Aristotle’s Posterior Analytics

In his Expositio libri Posteriorum Analyticorum (c. 1270–1272), Thomas Aquinas articulates his mature architecture of demonstrative science (scientia), taking geometry and arithmetic not merely as illustrative examples but as the primary realizations of the most certain mode of demonstration (certissimus modus demonstrationis, Lib. I, lect. 1). Because mathematical entities abstract from sensible matter and motion while retaining intelligible extension or discrete multitude, their demonstrations proceed solely from formal principles in which what is better known in relation to us (quoad nos) coincides with what is prior and better known according to nature (secundum naturam, Lib. I, lect. 4). Within this resolutory (analytica) framework, Aquinas resolves Plato‘s Meno paradox by distinguishing actual scientific knowledge (scire simpliciter) from the virtual pre-existence of conclusions within foreknown principles (praecognitio, Lib. I, lect. 2–3). Every demonstrative science requires three coordinated elements: a subject-genus (genus subiectum, such as continuous magnitude or number, of which both what it is and that it is are supposed), proper attributes (passiones, of which only nominal definitions are initially assumed while their inherence is demonstrated), and common axioms or dignities (dignitates, communes animi conceptiones, applied proportionally to the subject-genus, Lib. I, lect. 18). Crucially, Aquinas identifies Euclid‘s constructive problems—such as constructing an equilateral triangle upon a given finite straight line—as quasi-operative demonstrations (demonstrationes quasi operativae, Lib. I, lect. 2): in the order of construction the figure’s mathematical existence is first demonstrated as a proper attribute of prior spatial elements, after which the constructed figure serves as a secondary subject for subsequent theorems, while singular diagrams such as a figure inscribed in a semicircle immediately yield actual science once recognized under their universal species (Lib. I, lect. 2–3).

St. Thomas Aquinas, O.P. (1225–1274)

Commentary on Aristotle’s On the Soul

In the Sentencia libri De anima (c. 1267–1268), Thomas Aquinas provides the psychological and cognitive complement to the epistemological division of the sciences established in Super Boetium De Trinitate and the demonstrative logic of the Expositio libri Posteriorum Analyticorum. Whereas logic examines how geometric conclusions flow from immediate principles and metaphysics examines the ontological status of continuous quantity, psychology investigates the incarnate acts by which a human soul united to a body abstracts exact mathematical forms without forfeiting contact with spatial extension. Across the three books of the commentary, Aquinas traces an unbroken arc from the critique of Platonic-Pythagorean reifications of points and numbers (Book I, Lecture 11) and the physics of fluent being in sensory media (Book II, Lecture 16) to the mature doctrine of intelligible matter, turning to the phantasms, and the soul as an intellectual instrument analogous to the human hand (Book III, Lectures 8, 12, and 13).

English · Latin
St. Thomas Aquinas, O.P. (1225–1274)

Commentaries on Physics and On the Heavens

Across his mature commentaries on Aristotle‘s Physics and De Caelo (c. 1268–1273), Thomas Aquinas articulates a unified ontology and noetics of continuous quantity that bridges natural philosophy and pure geometry without collapsing either into Platonic separatism or empiricist atomism. The cornerstone of this synthesis is laid down in In II Physicorum, lectio 3: because dimensive quantity inheres in corporeal substance prior in the order of nature to sensible qualities and physical motion, the intellect can grasp points, lines, surfaces, and solids together with their per se attributes while leaving sensible matter out of consideration. Consequently, there is no falsehood in those who abstract (abstrahentium non est mendacium): the geometer does not judge that lines exist apart from physical bodies, but considers in isolation what is intelligibly prior to heat, weight, and resistance, retaining intelligible matter—the homogeneous three-dimensional extension that individuates congruent figures—while leaving behind sensible matter (unlike physical definitions, which resemble the snub in enclosing a determinate sensible subject, whereas mathematical definitions resemble the concave or curved). Conversely, the sciences intermediate between mathematics and physics—optics, harmonics, and astronomy—descend from pure mathematical demonstrations back to sensible matter, demonstrating physical conclusions through formal quantitative middle terms.

St. Thomas Aquinas, O.P. (1225–1274)

Commentary on Aristotle’s Metaphysics

Part V brings the entire Codex to its metaphysical and epistemological summit by uniting five foundational lectures from St. Thomas Aquinas’s Sententia super Metaphysicam (Books V, VII, and IX) with the systematic syntheses of the Summa Theologiae (Prima Pars, qq. 7, 12, 84, and 85), Quaestiones Disputatae de Potentia Dei (q. 7, a. 2), and Quaestiones Disputatae de Veritate (q. 10, a. 6). Whereas Parts I through IV charted the division of the speculative sciences, the apodictic structure of Euclidean demonstration, the psychological necessity of the spatial phantasm, and the physical ontology of the continuum, Part V grounds all of these doctrines in first philosophy. In Metaphysics V, lect. 9, Aquinas distinguishes real categorical being—contracted proportionally according to diverse modes of being (modi essendi) and predicating (modi praedicandi), with quantity following upon matter as the first intrinsic accident—from propositional being (esse as the verbal copula signifying the truth of judgment, illustrated by the falsity of the diagonal’s commensurability) and from the division of every category by potency and act (illustrated by the midpoint latent in potency within a continuous line). In Metaphysics VII, lect. 10–11, he resolves the Platonic-Pythagorean temptation to reduce geometric figures to pure numbers by establishing the hylomorphic analogy of mathematics: just as natural species require common sensible matter (flesh and bone) in their definition while abstracting from individual sensible matter, mathematical species such as the circle and the right angle dispense with sensible matter (bronze, stone, wood) yet require common materia intelligibilis (the continuum, lines, and surfaces) in their universal definition, while individual mathematical circles grasped in pure imagination are individuated by designated intelligible matter and posterior parts such as semicircles.
